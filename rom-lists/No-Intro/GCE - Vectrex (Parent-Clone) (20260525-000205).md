## Intent

A broad selection of the original controller-based Vectrex library. Mine Storm II, Star Castle, Scramble, Armor Attack and Cosmic Chasm provide the arcade foundation; Spike, Clean Sweep, Spinball and the more unusual games give it variety.

The machine's small library does not need an aggressive quota. Keep worthwhile games and qualified curiosities while excluding specialist accessories, prototypes and repeated versions. The 48-entry catalogue includes these alternatives and a BIOS ROM, rather than 48 different finished games.

## What earns a place

| Strand | Games | Why keep them |
|---|---|---|
| Arcade anchors | Mine Storm II, Star Castle, Scramble, Armor Attack, Berzerk | Strong starting points for vector-style movement, shooting and score chasing. |
| Distinctive shooting | Cosmic Chasm, Fortress of Narzod, Bedlam, Solar Quest | Different movement rules and threats rather than a single repeated space-shooter format. |
| Unusual action | WebWars, Spike, Clean Sweep | Flight and collection, character platforming, and a maze game change the pace. |
| Driving and pinball | Pole Position, HyperChase, Spinball | Another set of skills and a useful contrast to the shooters. |
| Combat and defence | Rip Off, Space Wars | Protecting fuel supplies and duelling another ship provide different objectives. |
| Qualified curiosities | Polar Rescue, Heads-Up, StarHawk, Star Trek | Submarine navigation, football and cockpit-view shooting broaden the collection, with more modest expectations. |

Polar Rescue is a qualified inclusion for its submarine instruments and rescue premise, not a recommendation as strong as Scramble. Heads-Up offers a computer opponent, so it can serve solo play. Space Wars also has a solo opponent, unlike the original arcade game's strictly head-to-head design; its appeal is still greater with another player.

Spike earns a place for character and a different style of play, rather than simply because its speech is a technical curiosity. HyperChase needs practice with its controls. Keeping these games provides an opportunity to explore the machine without claiming that every release is a hidden classic.

## Release decisions

- **Mine Storm II (Rev 2)** represents Mine Storm. It is the revised cartridge release associated with correcting the built-in game's progression problem, not an unrelated sequel. Do not duplicate it with the original Mine Storm entry.
- **Berzerk** and **Fortress of Narzod** use the European Rev 2 entries. One release per game is sufficient; retain the exact DAT names.
- **Star Trek - The Motion Picture** represents the game also released as **Star Ship**. Neither European Star Ship entry is added separately.
- **WebWars** represents the game also known as **WebWarp**. Its regional alternative is omitted.
- **Clean Sweep** is selected without also adding the Mr. Boston promotional variant.
- Pole Position uses its ordinary entry, not the Alt dump.
- No prototypes, betas, samples, test cartridges or BIOS images are selected. Dark Tower is held because this DAT identifies its release as a prototype, despite its reputation among enthusiasts.

## Accessories, controls and deliberate holds

The three **3D** games are held because their intended experience depends on the 3D Imager. AnimAction, Art Master and Melody Master belong to the light-pen side of the system and are also held. A conventional handheld controller should not inherit those requirements without a deliberate emulation plan.

Blitz! is omitted as a lower-priority sports choice, rather than because every sports game requires a second player. Heads-Up supplies football with a documented computer opponent. The selected Rip Off and Space Wars remain usable solo even though multiplayer is a substantial part of their appeal.

Preserve access to all four numbered controller buttons. HyperChase, for example, separates upshift, downshift, brake and accelerator; an automatic single-fire layout would lose essential actions. The original controller also has analogue movement, so driving and position-sensitive games deserve particular attention when assessing an emulator's handheld mapping.

The original machine draws bright lines on a portrait vector monitor, sometimes viewed through a coloured overlay. A small raster screen will render a different image. Aim for legible lines and the right proportions; an overlay can add context but should not hide the playfield. Curation does not imply that display and controls have been tested on the installed device.

## Deployment boundary

Keep the DAT filenames and their `.vec` extensions. The vecx core documents Vectrex image support, but a suitable NextUI emulator pak and its folder mapping remain to be confirmed. The README therefore records the curation with its deployment folder unassigned. No deployment script entry is enabled here.

These are single-file games; no disk playlists or cartridge-part assembly are needed. Firmware, rendering and accessory support remain properties of the chosen emulator rather than reasons to alter the verified game identities.

## Sources informing the choices

- [Original Vectrex manuals preserved by Digital Press](https://www.digitpress.com/library/manuals/vectrex/index.html): game controls and the separate light-pen and 3D hardware documentation.
- [Heads-Up instructions](https://www.digitpress.com/library/manuals/vectrex/heads_up.txt): computer opponent and solo controls.
- [Space Wars instructions](https://www.digitpress.com/library/manuals/vectrex/sp_wars.txt): player selection, thrust, firing and hyperspace.
- [HyperChase instructions](https://www.digitpress.com/library/manuals/vectrex/hyperchs.txt): its four separate driving actions.
- [Vectrex FAQ](https://www.vectrex.co.uk/files/articles/Vectrex%20FAQ%20v6-0.pdf): revised Mine Storm releases and regional aliases.
- [Digital Press, March/April 1992](https://www.digitpress.com/library/newsletters/digitalpress/dp4.pdf): firsthand retrospective recommendations and accessory distinctions.
- [vecx documentation](https://docs.libretro.com/library/vecx/): supported image types and controller buttons.
