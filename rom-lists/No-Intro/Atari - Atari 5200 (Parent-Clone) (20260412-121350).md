## Approach

A generous selection from a compact commercial library, with three completed later releases. Strong arcade conversions share space with the games that give the 5200 more identity: Space Dungeon, Rescue on Fractalus!, Star Raiders, Bounty Bob Strikes Back!, The Dreadnaught Factor and the expanded Pitfall II.

Much of the original commercial catalogue is retained. The aim is to offer worthwhile alternatives and discoveries rather than force this small library into an arbitrary quota. The reduction statistic includes the DAT's many prototypes, beta builds and diagnostic entries, not 207 distinct finished commercial games.

| Area | Selection intent |
|---|---|
| Arcade action | BerZerk, Defender, Centipede, Dig Dug, Joust, Moon Patrol and Wizard of Wor form a strong core. Gremlins, Pengo, QIX, Blue Print and Mr. Do!'s Castle add different rules and rhythms. |
| Shooting | Beamrider, River Raid, Gyruss, The Dreadnaught Factor, Zaxxon and Vanguard cover different approaches. Space Dungeon and Robotron add independent movement and firing rather than another conventional scrolling shooter. |
| Platforming and exploration | Bounty Bob Strikes Back!, Miner 2049er, H.E.R.O., Montezuma's Revenge, Mountain King and both Pitfall games offer substantial variety. Jungle Hunt, Kangaroo and Keystone Kapers supply shorter arcade challenges. |
| Cockpits and strategy | Rescue on Fractalus!, Star Raiders, Star Trek and Countermeasure offer different kinds of navigation and combat. Space Shuttle is a more demanding simulation discovery. |
| Puzzles, sports and precision | Zenji, QIX, Kaboom! and Super Breakout broaden the set beyond shooting. Ballblazer, Pole Position, RealSports Tennis and Decathlon provide representative sporting and racing play. |

Not every inclusion is an essential or the best version across all systems. Astro Chase, James Bond 007, Blue Print and the two Star Wars games are more qualified discoveries. The Dreadnaught Factor has an appealing giant-warship premise but unusual mission selection and demanding later challenges. Original friction is acknowledged rather than silently removed with hacks.

## Release choices and overlap

- Prefer ordinary USA commercial releases. Select **Gyruss Rev 1** rather than its earlier retail copy or beta. Alternate dumps are not extra games.
- **David Crane's Pitfall II: Lost Caverns** includes the second quest also associated with the Atari 8-bit version. This is a substantive reason to retain it alongside other systems' versions, not just another name for the same content.
- **Pac-Man** earns a place alongside **Ms. Pac-Man** here. The 5200 port is considered on its own merits rather than inheriting the reputation of the 2600 conversion.
- **Bounty Bob Strikes Back!** and **Miner 2049er** remain separate games. Both Frogger games and both Pitfall adventures likewise offer distinct experiences.
- **Carol Shaw's River Raid**, **BerZerk**, **MegaMania**, **QIX** and the other entries preserve their exact DAT spelling, capitalization and `.a52` filenames.
- Atari 8-bit computer relatives do not automatically replace the 5200 versions. These are console selections with their own controls and occasional content differences.
- BIOS images, diagnostic cartridges, demos, betas and prototypes are excluded. A prototype labelled Final is still not selected automatically.

## Completed later additions

Three releases are explicit exceptions to the original-commercial focus:

- **Tempest (AtariAge)** is the completed release finished by original programmer Keithen Hayenga. Select that entry rather than the unfinished prototype builds.
- **Koffi: Yellow Kopter** adds an original rescue-and-firefighting action game, with water collection and animal rescues giving it a different character from another arcade conversion.
- **Castle Crisis** adds Warlords-style castle defence against computer opponents as well as optional multiplayer. It is not dependent on having four people available to justify its place.

The unlicensed labels on Koffi and Castle Crisis are retained. These are deliberate completed-game choices, not a blanket inclusion of every community release. Castle Blast is omitted as a less compelling addition beside the selected precision and defence games.

## Controls and presentation

The 5200's analogue controller matters. **Missile Command**, **Centipede**, **Pole Position**, **Super Breakout**, **Kaboom!** and **Star Raiders** should not be assumed to feel correct under one universal digital mapping. They remain selected for their gameplay, with sensitivity and centring choices part of finding a comfortable setup. Their inclusion is not a promise that a D-pad feels like the original stick or Trak-Ball.

**Space Dungeon** and **Robotron** use two controllers for independent movement and firing. A handheld needs a deliberate arrangement for those actions. Their quality justifies retaining them rather than replacing them with simplified hacks.

The keypad, two fire actions and Start/Pause/Reset controls are part of the original interface. **Star Raiders** uses its keypad for ship systems and speed; **Space Shuttle** and other simulations also deserve their instructions. These are intentional learning and control requirements, not unexplained obstacles to launching the games.

Optional multiplayer in Ballblazer, Castle Crisis and other selections is welcome, but solo value is sufficient for their inclusion. The list does not assume multiple controllers or linked handhelds are available.

## Deliberate omissions

Congo Bongo and Meteorites do not earn places simply to make the original catalogue complete. Quest for Quintana Roo's awkwardness is less persuasive beside the selected adventures. RealSports Baseball, Football and Soccer are held in favour of the stronger and more distinctive sports representatives.

Interesting unfinished games—including The Last Starfighter, Blaster, Jr. Pac-Man, Millipede, Xari Arena, Super Pac-Man and Asteroids—are outside the default finished-release selection. Their absence is not a claim that no prototype is playable or worth studying; a prototype collection would be a separate editorial decision.

Later games outside this DAT need their own catalogue decision rather than invented filenames. Selection remains about worthwhile games and release choices, not the completeness of any particular set of available files.
